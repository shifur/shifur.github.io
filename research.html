Research

My research lies at the intersection of numerical analysis, scientific machine learning, Bayesian inverse problems and data assimilation. I develop computational methods for reliable learning and inference from limited data and expensive physical models, with applications to atmospheric and Earth system science.

Numerical approximation and scientific machine learning

I study reliable and computationally efficient surrogate models for expensive, nonlinear physical simulations, with a particular interest in learning high-dimensional parameter-to-response maps from limited training data. My current work compares compressed sensing and deep neural networks for idealized cloud microphysics models.

  • High-dimensional approximation, sparse polynomial expansions and compressed sensing.
  • Deep neural networks, weighted sparse recovery and square-root LASSO.
  • Numerical assessment of approximation errors, learning curves, multi-output model responses and computational efficiency.
  • Understanding when accurate forward-model approximations yield accurate Bayesian posteriors.

Active learning, sampling complexity and optimal observation design

I investigate how the choice and quantity of simulation samples and physical observations affect reliable learning and inverse problem recovery. My ongoing theoretical and numerical work examines the joint relationship between surrogate accuracy, information acquisition and the geometry of nonlinear inverse problems.

  • Sample-efficient learning, Christoffel-guided sampling and adaptive experimental design.
  • Joint simulation and observation complexity for surrogate-assisted Bayesian inference.
  • Identifiability, response-space geometry and preservation of informative measurement directions.
  • Optimal selection of cloud microphysics observations under limited measurement budgets.

Bayesian inverse problems, uncertainty quantification and data assimilation

I am interested in statistical and computational methods that combine observations with nonlinear physical models to estimate uncertain parameters and states. My current research uses surrogate-assisted Bayesian calibration to quantify cloud microphysics parameter uncertainty, and investigates how model approximation errors influence the reliability of inference.

  • Bayesian parameter estimation, posterior approximation and Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC).
  • Quantification of parameter and predictive uncertainty, including the effects of surrogate approximation.
  • Nonlinear and non-Gaussian inference, ensemble methods and the integration of AI with data assimilation.
  • Data assimilation and parameter estimation for coupled physical models, including collaborative wildfire reanalysis research.

Earth observation, wildfire modeling and reanalysis

My collaborative wildfire research connects multi-sensor Earth observations, physics-based modeling and scientific machine learning. My current work develops independent reference evidence and validation methods for satellite active-fire observations, while the broader project aims to reconstruct physically consistent wildfire histories and accelerate prediction.

  • Satellite fire detection, observation harmonization, spatiotemporal matching and native-footprint mapping using Landsat, VIIRS and MODIS.
  • Independent reference validation, sensor uncertainty characterization and multi-sensor fusion.
  • Data assimilation and ensemble parameter estimation for coupled fire and atmosphere models, supporting physically consistent wildfire reanalysis.
  • Physics-informed AI surrogates to accelerate parameter estimation and wildfire spread forecasting within the broader collaborative project.

Earlier research: Statistical time series and macroeconomic forecasting

My MSc dissertation at Queen Mary University of London, supervised by Dr. Matthew Lewis, compared UK inflation forecasting models using monthly RPI, unemployment and interest rate data (1988–2024). The analysis evaluated ARIMA, SARIMA, VAR, dynamic regression with ARIMA errors, and the Phillips Curve across economic regimes using held-out 12-month forecasts.

The study found that the best model depends on the economic period: ARIMA was strongest before the crisis, VAR after the crisis, and the Phillips Curve had the lowest errors over the full-period comparison. This motivates research on adaptive model selection and hybrid forecasting.
